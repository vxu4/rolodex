if i were to build a home after the fire

for pipe

if i were to build a home after the fire
i would start with the kitchen

i would buy a big wood table
off of facebook marketplace

i would start by making breakfast
looking out an imaginary window

i would make pasta in the ashes
of what was lost
cakes from the soil that’s left
and hope that you come have some

if i rebuild a home after the fire
i would stock the pantry
with all the candy our child selves craved
all the crunchiest treats and
no bread would go stale under the refrigerator light

if i rebuild our home after the fire
i will make our mother’s recipes on the stove
and the sauces would never turn bitter, or sour
even under the sun

this archive speaks to the trauma that my body holds but also to its ephemeral and incomplete nature. i don’t remember most of my childhood, i also have very few belongings to remind me that i was, indeed, a child: just a crocheted crossbody bag i used to wear and a little ceramic doll. the rest of my childhood is entrapped within the walls of my childhood home, a home i will likely never return to. it is, like the memories of my growing up, lost to me. this project attempts to be an archive of those ghosts, both material and metaphysical by returning to the one other thing i have left, the photos.

this archive is about the pitfalls and failures of rescuing memory, combined with the fantasies and possibilities of homemaking. bringing old family photographs together with AI, i attempt to recreate lost objects from my childhood home to form a wonky and incomplete patchwork of an imagined and impossible home.

the process is as follows: i gather photographs from interiors in my childhood: pictures of birthday parties, christmas present exchanges–  an everyday life i can’t really remember. i study them. sometimes a specific object sends a zap through my body… i recognize something. it is comforting to think not all is lost in me.

then, i use AI to “clean” the pictures. i click on the contours and bodies and erase them. the process is off putting, almost violent. what does it mean to erase oneself? one’s loved ones? i instead think of it more in terms of what is salvageable about these pictures to begin with, what is still real and material about them. the objects “rescued” from these old photographs often turn out incomplete and wonky. an image of a melting birthday cake, the leftover pattern of a smushed pillow, our 90s tv missing a piece as if someone took a bite from it. it is an archival exercise, speaking to what Julieta Singh calls “the ghost archive.”1 the exercise is indeed that, making sense of what is left, in the same way we put together fractured memories to make sense of our past, of what happened to us, and of the choices we made around them.

  1. The stories that comprise us have left us both wanting more, wishing we had access to a fuller narrative frame. I call this wishing-wanting desire "the ghost archive." Everything we need to know but cannot know as we keep circling and sniffing around the edges. Everything that keeps affecting us and affecting others through us. Everything that remains right there, but just out of reach. — Singh, The Ghost Archive, p. 96.
\

what would it be like to alchemize this lost archive into a material one? what would it be like to make a home out of these ghost objects? i imagine the relationships of these ghost objects to a home in the same way that traumatic memories2 exist in our bodies, jumbled and incoherent, both surreal and absurd, present yet never in full shape.

it is also an attempt at restoration of the archive.3 the restoration of a lost home, of a transgressed body. this archive is my desperate attempt at saying: “i existed, i was there, i will not abandon myself.”

in this house the memories collide into infinite, ridiculous possibilities. a pillow becomes a wall. a tablecloth paints the ceiling blue. nobody lives in the house yet it is fully alive with residues of lives past. but i fear that i am trying to theorize a feeling that is indeed quite simple: the building of the house4 is a dramatic gesture.5 an archive i can inhabit. a reclamation of a childhood lost. a place to finally live with ghosts.

  1. “[T]raumatic memories tend to arise as fragmented splinters of inchoate and indigestible sensations, emotions, images, smells, tastes, thoughts…jumbled fragments cannot be remembered in the narrative sense per se but are perpetually being replayed and reexperienced as unbidden and incoherent intrusions or physical symptoms.” — Levine, Trauma and Memory: Brain and Body in a Search for the Living Past, p. 7-8.
  2. “What is placed in or left out of the archive is a political act, dictated by the archivist and the political context in which she lives (...) Sometimes the proof is never committed to the archive—it is not considered important enough to record, or if it is, not important enough to preserve. Sometimes there is a deliberate act of destruction.” — Machado, In the Dream House, p. 13.
  3. “The house is not essential for domestic abuse, but hell, it helps: a private space where private dramas are enacted behind, as the cliché goes, closed doors; but also windows sealed against the sound, drawn curtains, silent phones. A house is never apolitical. It is conceived, constructed, occupied, and policed by people with power, needs, and fears. Windex is political. So is the incense you burn to hide the smell of sex, or a fight.” — Machado, In the Dream House, p. 76.
  4. “Dream House as Idiom. I always thought the expression “safe as houses” meant that houses were safe places. It’s a beautiful idea; like running home with a late-summer thunderstorm hung down your neck. There’s the house, waiting for you; a barrier from nature, from scrutiny, from other people. Standing on the other side of the glass, watching the sky playfully pummel the earth like a sibling. But house idioms and their variants, in fact, often signify the opposite of safety and security. If something is a house of cards it is precarious, easily disrupted. If the writing is on the wall we can see the end of something long before it arrives. If we do not throw stones in glass houses, it is because the house is constructed of hypocrisy, readily shattered. All expressions of weakness, of the inevitability of failure. “Safe as houses” is something closer to “the house always wins.” Instead of a shared structure providing shelter, it means that the person in charge is secure; everyone else should be afraid.”— Machado, In the Dream House, p. 78.

i spend a lot of time away from my body. at its worst, the pain of my past becomes so unbearable that i dissociate into a suicidal daze. yet all i do these days is to be a body. i sleep, i brush my teeth, i eat hospital food, i pace barefoot on the cold hallway, i get my vitals taken every morning. i am vitals and symptoms and body. i am learning to be a body again, as if i was trying to teach myself to walk. get out of bed, brush your teeth, feed yourself. like watering a plant just enough to keep it alive. to be in this body, away from the daze, is one of the hardest things. my trauma, my mistakes, my unheard apologies, all demand me to be away from this body, to dissociate6, to divorce myself from this body as one leaves a home.

how can this home be rebuilt and hospitable to life while full of absurdity and broken memories, in the same way that a traumatized body and mind can (and demand to) become a safe place again? if trauma has led me to a place of disembodiment7, healing is to embody, to become the body that has been given to you and so many times denied. healing is to rebuild the home and to inhabit it. in the house, the body finally renews itself into something to inhabit. a home.

  1. “While most survivors of childhood abuse describe a degree of proficiency in the use of trance, some develop a kind of dissociative virtuosity. They may learn to ignore severe pain, to hide their memories in complex amnesias, to alter their sense of time, place, or person, and to induce hallucinations or possession states. Sometimes these alterations of consciousness are deliberate, but often they become automatic and feel alien and involuntary.” — Levine, Trauma and Memory: Brain and Body in a Search for the Living Past, p. 102.
  2. “Embodiment is political, especially when many of us have been systematically disembodied on multiple fronts. To find peace within chaos, it’s an act of resistance, to accept the chaos, to move with it, to return home to a body that has never felt like yours requires so much patience that at times it almost feels easier to give up.” — Róisín, Who is Wellness For?, p. 132.
