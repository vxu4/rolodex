At a zenith in Hong Kong’s history emerged the development of The Sea Ranch where Seawitch now resides. A moment that was a mere decade since the riots within the colony and the deep upheavals across the border in the Mainland. Perhaps then, The Sea Ranch was built on the southern side of Lantau Island, rather than the north, east, or west to avoid the sight of refugees from the Mainland in pursuit of Hong Kong. Such a stain would ruin this sanctuary hidden from the wretched of the earth.

Yet, by the time that The Sea Ranch was completed in 1979, a different group of refugees emerged and disrupted the prized view of the southern horizon. Initially invisible due to their vast distance away from The Sea Ranch, the Vietnamese had gradually grown from being doubtful floaters in the gaze of the southern sea to being fully realized bodies and vessels that crashed, drowned, and emerged onto the islands that constitute Hong Kong. The refugees from Vietnam had landed on Lantau Island and were later placed in prison camps at Chi Ma Wan and Tong Fuk Miu Wan. Nearby, the refugees were also detained on the Soko Islands. Separated only by a short distance of hill, forest, and sea, The Sea Ranch was seemingly surrounded by the wretched of the earth.

A former Vietnamese refugee camp at Chi Ma Wan, Lantau Island

The Sea Ranch is now patinaed and bleached from decades of touch by crustaceans, humans and the airy spry of the briny sea. The aged private ferry that shuttles its residence to and from Cheung Chau had needed repair on the first weekend that I had arrived to Seawitch. The burbling ferry can be heard across the entire community, signaling to the residents to make haste for the journeys.

The Vietnamese had long left the perimeter of The Sea Ranch and in their place in these twilight days, the wretched have returned and even set foot in this once kettled place. Migrant workers from the Mainland have started to live in The Sea Ranch through an oblique arrangement with a local landlord who had converted once lavish flats into dense dormitories of bunk beds, cold industrial lighting, and a canteen. The workers, who are mostly men appearing in their twenties or fifties, make round-the-clock trips from their own private boat between The Sea Ranch and neighboring Shek Kwu Chau island to build a waste incinerator plant. Their leisurely hours are spent fishing by the pier of The Sea Ranch and video calling family and friends from the balconies of The Sea Ranch. When the workers head out to Cheung Chau, they bring back cartons of Coca Cola and Snow Beer. Even without their industrial clothing, the workers in their leisurely atire stand out in their appearance from the long-term residents of The Sea Ranch. A gem of colonial Hong Kong has become the staging ground for the waste disposable of the contemporary special administrative region. A place once a symbol of distinction and difference from the masses in colony and the Mainland has become a place among the masses.
